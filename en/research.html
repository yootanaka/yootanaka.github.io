How does the genome shape morphology?

Animal form arises through development, beginning with the fertilized egg. During development, many genes are expressed to control cell behavior, and when and where each of these developmental regulatory genes is expressed is determined by combinations of diverse elements in the genome.

Using genetic engineering in fish and amphibian model organisms, mainly zebrafish (Danio rerio), medaka (Oryzias latipes) and clawed frog (Xenopus), I study how developmental regulatory genes are controlled and how changes in that control give rise to morphological evolution. Beyond functional analyses within these model organisms, I aim to introduce genetic information unique to other fishes and amphibians that are not amenable to genetic engineering into model organisms, and thereby induce the corresponding morphological changes in vivo.

Cis- and trans-regulatory changes associated with morphological evolution

The regulation of developmental genes falls broadly into two types: regulation by DNA sequences near the gene, such as enhancers (cis-regulation), and regulation by transcription factors that bind to those enhancers (trans-regulation). I identify cis- and trans-regulatory changes that arose together with morphological evolution and test their effects on morphology in vivo. As a first step, I also observe and analyze the morphology of fishes and amphibians myself to determine where on the phylogenetic tree each morphological change occurred. My current focus is on changes in developmental regulators that accompanied the evolution of the fin skeleton in fishes.

Mechanisms of functional divergence of genes after whole-genome duplication

Whole-genome duplication (WGD) is the doubling of an organism's entire genetic information (its genome). The ancestor of vertebrates is thought to have undergone two rounds of WGD, producing the template of the vertebrate genome that we humans also carry. Among fishes and amphibians, several lineages have independently undergone a third or further round of WGD, suggesting that aquatic vertebrates are unusually tolerant of whole-genome duplication. Using fishes and amphibians that have experienced WGD, I investigate how genomes actually evolve after duplication.

Synteny and the mechanisms of morphogenesis

Synteny refers to genomic regions in which the relative positions of genes are conserved across species. By altering syntenic arrangements with genome editing, it is possible to affect morphogenesis without directly disrupting the function of any gene. Using genome editing in fishes and amphibians, I study how synteny and other aspects of the relative positions of genes influence development and morphogenesis.

Developing and improving genetic engineering tools

Pursuing these research topics often runs up against the technical limits of current genetic engineering methods, so I also develop new techniques for fishes and amphibians myself. I am currently developing and improving gene expression systems based on CRISPR and a morning glory–derived transposon, as well as methods for engineering synteny.
